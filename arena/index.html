How does a decision engine play Tetris?

Code lists every landing spot and does the counting. Each spot is described in plain English — “The piece leaves no holes under it in the middle, sits flat on the surface, and the stack stays low.” — and the engine answers one typed yes/no question, “Does the stack look clean?”. The piece goes to the highest P(clean), lowest index on ties. This is the exact laya-tetris-v2 grammar the arena tables were measured on; the fitted modelless head in those tables needs a corpus per game — the out-of-the-box engine you are watching honestly abstains here and falls back to a random spot.

Why ask what it sees, not what to do?

The game writes where the bird is — “The bird is a little below the gap center, falling. The gap is narrow. The pipe is just ahead.” — and asks one typed question per action: “Will the bird pass through the gap cleanly?” over the frozen v2 option sentences. The bird does the action with the higher P(clean). Asked which way to move, decision models answer backwards; asked where things are, they read cleanly.

Does wording matter?

A lot. Each barrier row is described in plain English and the engine answers “Is this lane safe to run?” per lane. The runner takes the lane with the highest P(safe). Small wording changes move the probabilities a lot; the grammar here is the measured, pinned one.

With your engine connected, every number on this page is produced live by the two lanes on your machine — nothing is scripted or pre-recorded.
