How each lane picks a spot

Same game for everyone (seeded 7-bag, hard drop, guideline scoring) — only the pick differs. Source: tetris_lane_flows.md. Press play on any figure to walk one recorded turn of the seed-607 game — every walk's input and output is replayed from the recorded games, never typed.

  2. 2 · List every spot

    The harness enumerates every legal landing: each rotation of the piece in each column, hard-dropped. For the opening O piece that is nine distinct futures — the count rides the recorded turn above.

  3. 3 · Say it in words

    Each spot becomes one English sentence — the holes it leaves, how it meets the surface, where it lands, how tall the stack stands. The words are pinned by the grammar; every lane reads the same ones.

  4. 4 · The model reads

    The laya model runs one forward pass per sentence. Rust is the served port; Python is the torch reference it is measured against — the same open weights either way.

  5. 5 · P(clean) per spot

    Every landing spot comes back with a cleanliness probability. The recorded opening turn's nine answers are below — watch how flat they are on an empty board: the model is nearly indifferent, and small differences decide the pick.

  2. 2 · Chain the preview piece

    Each candidate board is paired with every landing spot of the preview piece — and the tree is pruned hard: only the best six boards survive each level, so the search stays tiny instead of exploding.

  3. 3 · Cover the unknown third piece

    The piece after that is not known yet. Rather than gamble on a single guess, the plan plays out every piece the bag could still deal — the search branches over all of them.

  4. 4 · Score every end board

    Each final board is scored by the strategy rulebook — holes, bumpiness, stack height, wells, lines cleared — the instincts of a careful human player, written as numbers.

  5. 5 · Average over the unknown · pick the plan

    A plan is only as good as its worst realistic draw: scores are averaged across the possible third pieces, so a line-clear that needs the I-piece rates low — it usually does not come. The sturdiest plan wins.

  5. 1 · 2a · 2b · One search, three weights

    The modes are not three different AIs — it is the same placement search wearing different score weights: self-evolved builder weights while safe, proven survival weights in trouble. That is the whole trick behind the rulebook lane.

  2. 2 · Corpus engine

    With no heads, the engine can only compare the sentence against its corpus. The spot sentences of a real game are almost all new — so this branch is the one the recorded raw game took, five hundred and fifteen times in a row.

  3. 3a · In corpus → play the highest

    When a sentence lands close to something the corpus holds, the engine answers like laya would and the highest-probability neighbour wins. In the whole recorded raw game this path never fired — that honesty is what makes it the floor.

  4. 3b · Off corpus → abstain, play random

    Every outcome comes back null — the engine abstains rather than guess. The abstain plays a random legal spot from a separate seeded stream, labelled as random in the replay so nothing pretends to be a decision.

Why ask what it sees, not what to do?

The game writes where the bird is — “The bird is a little below the gap center, falling. The gap is narrow. The pipe is just ahead.” — and asks one typed question per action: “Will the bird pass through the gap cleanly?” over the frozen v2 option sentences. The bird does the action with the higher P(clean). Asked which way to move, decision models answer backwards; asked where things are, they read cleanly.

Does wording matter?

A lot. Each barrier row is described in plain English and the engine answers “Is this lane safe to run?” per lane; the runner takes the highest P(safe). Even the noun moves the answer (“a barrier” vs “a train” for the same lane), so the grammar pins ONE noun per obstacle class — and the fitted head absorbs the rest.

What's live, what's recorded, and how it is checked
  • With your engine connected, every board except laya (Python) plays live on your machine; the page reaches only 127.0.0.1:7331.
  • Without an engine, Reflex · modelless plays live in-tab from compiled WebAssembly heads, each parity-checked against the recorded engine play before any board moves. arena_head_parity.mjs — Tetris bit-exact, Flappy and three-lanes pinned to their published agreement
  • Reflex · rulebook runs the public reflexer engine as WebAssembly — in this tab and on a Cloudflare Worker (stateless, no storage). Both replay the recorded walk pick-for-pick. reflexer_parity.mjs gist-rs/riir-reflexer

  • laya (Rust) is our Rust port of the open-weights model, G5-parity-gated against its reference checkpoints; no Python ships anywhere. laya (Python) is that reference, recorded offline for comparison only. laya_riir_parity.rs laya
