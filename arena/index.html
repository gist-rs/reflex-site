Why ask what it sees, not what to do?

The game writes where the bird is — “The bird is a little below the gap center, falling. The gap is narrow. The pipe is just ahead.” — and asks one typed question per action: “Will the bird pass through the gap cleanly?” over the frozen v2 option sentences. The bird does the action with the higher P(clean). Asked which way to move, decision models answer backwards; asked where things are, they read cleanly.

Does wording matter?

A lot. Each barrier row is described in plain English and the engine answers “Is this lane safe to run?” per lane. The runner takes the lane with the highest P(safe). Small wording changes move the probabilities a lot — the measured trap here was noun choice itself (the same lane blocked by “a barrier” vs “a train” reads 0.75 vs 0.45), so the grammar carries ONE noun per obstacle class, never varied — and the fitted head absorbs the residual noun-dependent weights from the corpus, which is exactly why it beats the clearest-lane arithmetic policy at imitating the model (84% vs 74%). The grammar here is the measured, pinned one.

With your engine connected, every number on this page is produced live by the lanes on your machine — nothing is scripted or pre-recorded. Without an engine, the modelless Tetris, Flappy and three-lanes boards play live in-tab on the compiled WebAssembly heads (tetris parity-proven against the recorded engine play; flappy and lanes reproducing their published agreements, before any board moves), while the other boards replay recorded games — committed runs through this same wire, labelled as such, never mixed with live results. The laya (Rust) lane runs our Rust port of the open-weights model — G5-parity-verified against its reference checkpoints (top-1 agreement 1.000, probability drift ≤ 3e-6); there is no Python anywhere in the shipped stack. The laya (Python) board is that reference itself, recorded offline for comparison — it never runs in the engine or on this page. Protocol and measurement tables: the benchmark.
